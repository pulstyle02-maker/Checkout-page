I. Basic & Clinical Virology

1. General Virology Principles & Structure

Comparison: Viruses vs. Cells

Viruses are not cells; they are noncellular particles containing an internal core with either DNA or RNA, but not both, encased by a protective protein coat (capsid). They do not possess a nucleus, cytoplasm, mitochondria, or ribosomes. Cells (both prokaryotic and eukaryotic) possess both DNA and RNA.

  • Independent replication: Cells multiply by binary fission or mitosis. Viruses replicate by assembly; one parental virion enters and gives rise to hundreds of progeny virions.
  • Obligate intracellular parasites: Because viruses synthesize neither their own energy nor their own proteins, they must reproduce inside cells. (The only bacteria that are obligate intracellular parasites are Chlamydiae and Rickettsiae).
  • Diploid vs. Haploid: Almost all viruses are haploid (single copy of genome). Retroviruses are the sole exception; they are diploid (two copies of RNA genome).

Viral Capsid, Symmetry & Envelope

  • Size & Shape: Diameters span from 20 nm (size of large protein) up to 300 nm (size of smallest cell). Capsomers arranged in geometric symmetry build the capsid.
  • Symmetries:
    • Icosahedral: 20 equilateral triangular faces approximating a sphere. Can be naked (nonenveloped) or enveloped.
    • Helical: Capsomers wound around a hollow coil. All human helical viruses are enveloped (there are no naked helical viruses that infect humans).
  • Envelope: Outer membrane acquired by budding through host cellular membranes (mostly plasma membrane; herpesviruses uniquely bud from the inner nuclear membrane). Matrix protein mediates interaction between envelope and capsid.
    • Stability: Enveloped viruses are sensitive to heat, drying, detergents, and lipid solvents (alcohol, ether). Transmitted mainly by direct contact (blood, sexual, secretions) or droplets.
    • Nonenveloped (Naked) Viruses: More stable in the environment. Typically transmitted via the fecal-oral route (e.g., poliovirus, hepatitis A virus, rotavirus, norovirus). Rhinoviruses are naked respiratory viruses capable of surviving on fomites.
  • Tegument: Found uniquely in herpesviruses (HSV, CMV) located between nucleocapsid and envelope. Houses regulatory proteins including transcription factors (e.g., VP-16) and immune evaders.

Atypical Virus-Like Agents

  • Defective Viruses: Composed of viral nucleic acid and proteins, but cannot replicate without a helper virus providing the missing gene function (e.g., Hepatitis D virus requires Hepatitis B virus). Produced in excess (up to 100:1 defective to infectious particles).
  • Pseudovirions: Contain host cell DNA instead of viral DNA inside the viral capsid; can penetrate host cells but cannot replicate.
  • Viroids: Small, circular single-stranded RNA molecules without a protein coat or envelope; do not encode proteins. Cause plant diseases, no known human diseases.
  • Prions: Infectious particles consisting solely of protein with no detectable nucleic acid.
    • Pathogenesis: Encoded by a normal cellular gene (PrPC, predominantly alpha-helical). Undergoes posttranslational conformation change into beta-pleated sheets (PrPSC). PrPSC recruits normal PrPC to change shape, aggregating into protease-resistant, amyloid-like filaments.
    • Properties: Highly resistant to boiling, heat, ultraviolet light, formaldehyde, and nucleases. Inactivated by hypochlorite, 1 N NaOH, and prolonged autoclaving.
    • Immunology: Because they are modified host cellular proteins, they induce no antibody response and no inflammatory response. Produces spongiform encephalopathies (vacuolated, holes in brain tissue, neuronal death).

Genome Organization & mRNA Strategies

  • DNA Viruses: All have double-stranded DNA except Parvoviruses (single-stranded linear DNA). All replicate in the nucleus except Poxviruses (replicate in cytoplasm and carry their own DNA-dependent RNA polymerase). All are linear except Papillomaviruses, Polyomaviruses (circular supercoiled), and Hepadnaviruses (circular incomplete double-strand).
  • RNA Viruses: All have single-stranded RNA except Reoviruses/Rotaviruses (double-stranded segmented RNA). All replicate in the cytoplasm except Retroviruses, Influenza virus, and Hepatitis D virus (which have nuclear steps).
    • Positive (+) Polarity RNA: Base sequence matches mRNA. Genome can function directly as mRNA in host ribosomes. Purified (+) ssRNA is infectious (e.g., Poliovirus). (Exception: Retroviruses carry reverse transcriptase in virion; retroviral RNA is not infectious alone, but proviral DNA is).
    • Negative (−) Polarity RNA: Base sequence is complementary to mRNA. Virions must contain an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase to synthesize mRNA. Purified naked genome is noninfectious. Examples: Influenza, Measles, Rabies.
    • Ambisense RNA: Arenaviruses and some bunyaviruses contain regions of both positive and negative polarity on the same RNA segment.
  • Infectious Nucleic Acid: Purified viral DNA or RNA free of all proteins that can establish complete productive infection when introduced into host cells, bypassing cell-surface receptor specificity.
  • Precursor Polyprotein Cleavage: Picornaviruses, Flaviviruses, Coronaviruses, and Retroviruses translate long polyproteins that require proteolytic cleavage by virus-encoded proteases to yield active structural and functional proteins.

Lysogeny & Transduction

Lysogeny: State in which temperate bacteriophage DNA circularizes and integrates into the bacterial chromosome as a prophage. Governed by a repressor protein (c-I gene product) versus its antagonizer (cro gene product). Inducing agents (UV light) trigger proteases that cleave the repressor, leading to prophage excision and lytic replication.
Lysogenic Conversion: Bacterial acquisition of new phenotypic traits (e.g., exotoxin production) mediated by prophage genes. Examples: Diphtheria toxin (C. diphtheriae), Botulinum toxin (C. botulinum), Cholera toxin (V. cholerae), Erythrogenic toxin (S. pyogenes), Shiga toxin (E. coli O157).
Transduction: Gene transfer between bacterial cells mediated by bacteriophages.

Viral Genetics: Interactions Between Viruses

Phenomenon Mechanism Key Clinical / Biological Example
Recombination Exchange of genes via crossing over between homologous regions of dsDNA genomes. (Rare in RNA viruses). Genetic mapping of herpesviruses, adenoviruses.
Reassortment Exchange of intact genome segments in viruses with segmented genomes infecting the same cell. Antigenic Shift in Influenza A virus (causing worldwide pandemics); Rotavirus reassortant vaccines.
Complementation One virus supplies an active protein product that rescues a replication-defective or mutated virus. Hepatitis B virus supplies HBsAg to defective Hepatitis D virus; helper retroviruses in gene therapy.
Phenotypic Mixing Genome of virus A is coated with surface proteins of virus B (or mixed surface mosaic). Progeny of the subsequent infection reverts entirely to genome A genotype and coat. Pseudotypes (e.g., vesicular stomatitis virus genome expressing SARS-CoV-2 spike protein).

3. Host Defenses, Laboratory Diagnosis & Antivirals

Innate Nonspecific Host Defenses
  • Interferons (IFN-α and IFN-β): Type I interferons, induced primarily by viruses and double-stranded RNA (dsRNA). They bind cell receptors on neighboring uninfected cells, signaling transcription of three inactive enzymes:
    1. 2,5-Oligo A Synthetase: Activated by dsRNA to make 2,5-oligo A.
    2. Ribonuclease (RNase L): Activated by 2,5-oligo A; cleaves and degrades cellular and viral mRNA.
    3. Protein Kinase: Phosphorylates and inactivates elongation initiation factor-2 (eIF-2), arresting protein synthesis. The cell dies, preventing viral spread.
  • Natural Killer (NK) Cells: Lymphocytes lacking specific antigen receptors; recognize and kill virus-infected cells displaying reduced or absent Class I MHC proteins via perforins and granzymes.
  • Other Innate Factors: Macrophages (phagocytosis), α-defensins (block HIV CXCR4 binding), APOBEC3G (deaminates cytosines causing retroviral DNA hypermutation; neutralized by HIV Vif protein), mucociliary escalator, fever.
Adaptive Specific Host Defenses
  • Active Immunity: Neutralizing antibodies (directed against surface spikes/capsid proteins, blocking adherence or uncoating) and Cytotoxic CD8+ T cells (recognize viral peptide on Class I MHC; kill infected cells via perforins, granzymes, and Fas/FasL apoptosis).
  • Secretory IgA: Protects mucosal surfaces against respiratory and GI viruses. Serum IgG neutralizes viruses during viremia and systemic spread.
  • Passive Immunity: Preformed antibodies (hyperimmune globulins for rabies, HBV, VZV; pooled IgG for HAV, measles) affording immediate but temporary protection (weeks to months).
  • Herd Immunity: Interruption of transmission protecting unimmunized individuals when high percentages (≥60–90%) are immune. Attained with live vaccines inducing mucosal IgA (e.g., Oral Polio Vaccine) or natural infection, but not by killed polio vaccines.

4. DNA Enveloped Viruses

Herpes Simplex Virus 1 & 2 (HSV-1, HSV-2) DNA Enveloped

  • Distinction: HSV-1 primarily infects above the waist (saliva transmission; latently lodged in the trigeminal ganglia). HSV-2 infects below the waist (sexual transmission; latently lodged in lumbar and sacral ganglia). Oral-genital sexual practices lead to cross-site infection in 10–20%.
  • Replication & Latency: Binds heparan sulfate and nectin; uncoats and transports DNA into nucleus where it circularizes as an unintegrated episome. Immediate-early transcription initiated by virion tegument protein VP-16. In neurons, VP-16 fails to activate IE transcription; latency-associated transcripts (LATS) suppress replication. Linear concatenates replicated by rolling circle and cleaved by viral terminase into virions. Buds from the inner nuclear membrane.
  • Clinical Syndromes:
    • HSV-1: Gingivostomatitis in children (fever, irritability, oral vesicles); herpes labialis (cold sores at mucocutaneous border); keratoconjunctivitis (corneal dendritic branching ulcers); temporal lobe encephalitis (fever, headache, seizures, focal temporal necrosis, high mortality); herpetic whitlow (fingers); herpes gladiatorum (wrestlers); eczema herpeticum (atopic dermatitis patients).
    • HSV-2: Painful vesicular genital and anal ulcerations; aseptic meningitis; neonatal herpes (severe encephalitis, disseminated infection contracted via contact with vesicular lesions or asymptomatic shedding in birth canal).
    • Erythema Multiforme: Target/bull’s eye cutaneous lesions on limbs and trunk linked immunologically to HSV antigens. Severe form: Stevens-Johnson syndrome.
  • Diagnosis: PCR/NAAT for viral DNA (gold standard); Tzanck smear showing multinucleated giant cells with intranuclear inclusions; culture in 1–3 days.
  • Treatment: Acyclovir (IV for encephalitis and neonatal infections; oral for mucosal/genital episodes). Valacyclovir and famciclovir for oral suppression. Does not eradicate latency. Foscarnet for acyclovir-resistant TK mutants.

Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV) DNA Enveloped

  • Pathogenesis: Transmitted by respiratory droplets or vesicle direct contact. Initial mucosal infection spreads via blood to skin producing chickenpox; ascends sensory nerves via retrograde flow to establish lifetime latency in dorsal root or cranial sensory ganglia (circular, unintegrated DNA).
  • Clinical Findings:
    • Varicella (Chickenpox): 14–21 day incubation; prodrome of fever/malaise followed by intensely pruritic, pleomorphic papulovesicular rash in "crops" (papules → vesicles → pustules → crusts), primarily on trunk then face and limbs. Complications: adult interstitial pneumonia, encephalitis, bacterial superinfection, Reye's syndrome (encephalopathy/fatty liver in children taking aspirin).
    • Zoster (Shingles): Reactivation along unilateral sensory dermatomes of head or thorax; intense burning pain, followed by crops of vesicles. Postherpetic neuralgia (severe intractable pain persisting weeks to months). Immunocompromised suffer life-threatening visceral dissemination.
  • Diagnosis: PCR on vesicular fluid; DFA stain; Tzanck smear (multinucleated giant cells).
  • Treatment: Acyclovir (high-dose oral/IV), valacyclovir, famciclovir. Foscarnet for resistant strains.
  • Vaccines:
    • Varivax: Live, attenuated VZV vaccine for children ≥12 months. (Contraindicated in pregnancy/immunocompromise).
    • Shingrix: Recombinant subunit vaccine containing glycoprotein E with AS01 adjuvant (lipid A + saponin) for adults. Highly effective; safe in immunocompromised. (Live Zostavax withdrawn).

Cytomegalovirus (CMV) DNA Enveloped

  • Pathogenesis & Immune Evasion: Latent within monocytes and macrophage precursors. Destabilizes Class I MHC-peptide complexes, produces microRNAs that block Class I MHC mRNA translation, and secretes a chemokine receptor homologue that scavenges host chemokines. Replicates in nucleus; assembly involves capsids loading DNA through portal proteins cleaved by viral terminase.
  • Transmission: Saliva, sexual (semen, cervical mucus), blood transfusions, organ transplants, transplacental, perinatally in birth canal, breast milk.
  • Clinical Manifestations:
    • Congenital Cytomegalic Inclusion Disease: Leading congenital cause of infectious mental retardation and sensorineural deafness. Features: microcephaly, intracranial calcifications, seizures, jaundice, hepatosplenomegaly, "blueberry muffin" purpuric skin lesions. Most damage occurs when mother acquires primary infection in first trimester.
    • Immunocompetent Adults: Heterophil-negative mononucleosis (fever, lethargy, atypical lymphocytosis).
    • Immunocompromised (AIDS / Transplant Recipients): Retinitis ("pizza-pie" hemorrhage/exudates causing blindness), intractable colitis, esophagitis, interstitial pneumonitis. Immune Reconstitution Inflammatory Syndrome (IRIS) occurs if HAART is started prematurely.
  • Diagnosis: PCR/NAAT for viral DNA; rapid shell vial culture; histological discovery of large cells with prominent intranuclear "owl's eye" inclusion bodies; detection of pp65 viral nucleocapsid protein within blood leukocytes by immunofluorescence.
  • Treatment & Prophylaxis: Ganciclovir (drug of choice; activated by CMV UL97 kinase), Valganciclovir, Cidofovir, Foscarnet. Fomivirsen (antisense DNA) for intraocular retinitis. Letermovir inhibits terminase for prophylaxis in stem-cell transplants. Cytomegalovirus hyperimmune globulin (CytoGam) for organ transplants.

Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) DNA Enveloped

  • Transmission & Tropism: Saliva ("kissing disease"). Binds to the CD21 receptor (complement C3d receptor) on human B lymphocytes and infects oropharyngeal epithelial cells. Becomes latent as circular unintegrated episomes inside memory B cells.
  • Infectious Mononucleosis: Fever, sore throat, generalized lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly (risk of rupture), hepatitis. Peripheral blood reveals absolute lymphocytosis with atypical lymphocytes (enlarged CD8+ cytotoxic T cells attacking infected B cells).
  • Associated Malignancies & Pathologies:
    • Burkitt's Lymphoma: Endemic in East African children. Translocation of c-myc proto-oncogene from chromosome 8 to chromosome 14 near active immunoglobulin heavy chain promoter.
    • Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma: Prevalent in southern China.
    • Other: Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, Post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD; regresses on reducing immunosuppression), Oral Hairy Leukoplakia (white plaques on lateral tongue margin in AIDS), Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH).
    • X-linked Lymphoproliferative Syndrome: Fatal progressive infection in boys with mutated T/NK signaling gene.
  • Diagnosis: Heterophil antibody detection via Monospot test (agglutination of horse/sheep erythrocytes adsorbed by guinea pig kidney extract). EBV-specific serology: IgM to viral capsid antigen (VCA) indicates acute infection; IgG VCA and EBNA indicate past infection.
  • Treatment: Supportive. Acyclovir is clinically ineffective.

Human Herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6) & Human Herpesvirus 8 (HHV-8) DNA Enveloped

  • Human Herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6B): Lymphotropic for CD4+ T cells. Etiologic agent of Roseola infantum (exanthem subitum) in infants 6 months to 3 years: abrupt high fever for 3–5 days, which resolves, followed immediately by an erythematous maculopapular rash on the trunk and face. Latent in lymphocytes; reactivates as encephalitis/pneumonitis in stem-cell transplant recipients. Ganciclovir used in severe immunocompromised cases.
  • Human Herpesvirus 8 (HHV-8 / KSHV): Transmitted by saliva and sexual intercourse. Replicates latently in vascular endothelial cells and B lymphocytes.
    • Malignancy Mechanisms: Latency-Associated Nuclear Antigen (LANA) inactivates host tumor suppressor proteins p53 and Rb. Contains pirated human gene homologues for cyclin D, IL-6, and vFLIP (blocks apoptosis). Downregulates Class I MHC via MIR-1 and MIR-2.
    • Clinical Features: Kaposi's sarcoma (KS): reddish-purple flat macules, plaques, or nodular spindle-cell tumors forming microvascular slits in skin, oral cavity, GI tract, and lungs in AIDS patients and transplant recipients. Primary effusion lymphoma and Multicentric Castleman's disease (MCD). KSHV-associated inflammatory cytokine syndrome (KICS).
    • Treatment: Antiretroviral therapy (ART) for AIDS patients; liposomal doxorubicin/daunorubicin, intralesional vinblastine, or radiation. Conventional anti-herpes drugs (acyclovir) are ineffective against KS lesions.

Poxviruses: Smallpox & Molluscum Contagiosum DNA Enveloped

  • Poxvirus Features: Largest, brick-shaped viruses with complex symmetry. Contain linear dsDNA and a virion-associated DNA-dependent RNA polymerase (essential because they replicate exclusively in the host cell cytoplasm).
  • Smallpox (Variola) Virus: Eradicated worldwide in 1977. High fever, centrifugal rash progressing through synchronous stages (macules → papules → vesicles → pustules → scars).
    • Factors Enabling Eradication: Single stable serotype, no animal reservoir, clinical visibility (no inapparent infections or carrier state), prompt immunity from live vaccinia vaccine, long-lived protection. Ring vaccination protects exposed contacts up to 4 days postexposure.
    • Treatment/Therapy: Tecovirimat (inhibits viral p37 protein needed for virion egress). Vaccinia immune globulin (VIG) for vaccination complications.
  • Molluscum Contagiosum Virus (MCV): Transmitted by direct personal or sexual contact. Causes multiple painless, nonpruritic, flesh-colored, dome-shaped papules with a distinct central umbilicated crater containing a curd-like white plug. Histology shows large cytoplasmic eosinophilic inclusion bodies (molluscum bodies). Treated with curettage, liquid nitrogen, or topical cidofovir.
  • Zoonotic Animal Poxviruses: Cowpox, Pseudocowpox (milker's nodules), Orf (contagious pustular dermatitis from sheep). Monkeypox (Central Africa, prairie dog vector in US 2003) causes smallpox-like illness with 1–10% mortality; protected partially by smallpox vaccine.

Hepatitis B Virus (HBV) DNA Enveloped

  • Virion Structure & Genome: 42-nm Dane particle; icosahedral core containing partially double-stranded circular DNA and virion DNA polymerase with both DNA-dependent and RNA-dependent (reverse transcriptase) activity. Excess noninfectious 22-nm spheres and filaments composed solely of HBsAg circulate in serum (1000:1 ratio).
  • Antigens:
    • HBsAg: Envelope surface antigen; indicates active infection (acute or carrier); immunogen in recombinant vaccine.
    • HBcAg: Nucleocapsid core antigen; non-secreted; induces non-neutralizing antibodies.
    • HBeAg: Cleaved derivative of core antigen released into blood; high indicator of active replication, high viral load, and transmissibility.
  • Unique Replicative Cycle: Host DNA polymerase completes strand inside nucleus into covalently closed circular DNA (CCC DNA). CCC DNA minus strand transcribes full-length RNA pregenome. In cytoplasm, viral reverse transcriptase copies pregenome RNA into progeny minus-strand DNA and then positive-strand DNA inside newly assembled capsids.
  • Transmission: Blood-borne (IV drug abuse, transfusions, needle-sticks), sexual, and perinatal (during birth). Neonates have a 90% chronic carriage rate.
  • Pathogenesis: Noncytocidal; damage to hepatocytes mediated by host cytotoxic T cells attacking viral antigens on surface. Chronic carrier state (>6 months of HBsAg) leads to cirrhosis and Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC). HBx protein inhibits p53 tumor suppressor.

HBV Treatment & Prophylaxis:
  • Treatment: Entecavir or Tenofovir (reverse transcriptase inhibitors; oral); Peginterferon alfa-2a. Does not eradicate latent CCC DNA in nuclei. High risk of reactivation under anti-CD20 (rituximab) or stem-cell transplants requires prophylactic tenofovir/entecavir.
  • Prevention: Recombinant yeast subunit vaccine containing HBsAg (Recombivax, Engerix-B, Heplisav-B). Prevents HCC. Passive-active immunity with HBIG and vaccine given within 12 hours of birth to neonates of HBsAg-positive mothers or after needle-sticks.

5. DNA Nonenveloped (Naked) Viruses

Human Papillomavirus (HPV) DNA Nonenveloped

  • Properties: Small, nonenveloped icosahedral capsid containing circular double-stranded supercoiled DNA. 100+ serotypes. Replicates in basal cells via host DNA polymerase; progeny virions mature only in terminally differentiated keratinized squamous cells on skin/mucosa.
  • Oncogenesis: In malignant transformation, viral DNA covalently integrates into host genome, inactivating early gene E2 and causing unchecked overexpression of oncoproteins:
    • E6 oncoprotein: Binds and recruits ubiquitin ligase to destroy tumor suppressor p53.
    • E7 oncoprotein: Binds and inactivates tumor suppressor protein Rb (retinoblastoma).
  • Clinical Manifestations:
    • Cutaneous Warts: Verruca vulgaris and plantar warts (HPV types 1, 2, 3, 4).
    • Genital Warts (Condylomata Acuminata): Benign cauliflower-like anogenital warts; also childhood laryngeal papillomas (HPV types 6 and 11).
    • Malignancies: Carcinoma of cervix, penis, anus, vulva, and oropharyngeal carcinomas (High-risk HPV types 16 and 18; also 31, 33, 45, 52, 58).
  • Diagnosis: Pap smear or cervical biopsy showing koilocytes (vacuolated squamous epithelial cells with pyknotic nuclei and clear halos); PCR assays for high-risk HPV DNA types.
  • Treatment & Prevention: Liquid nitrogen cryotherapy, surgical excision, topical imiquimod or podophyllin. Cidofovir for severe recalcitrant disease. Prevention: Gardasil 9 (recombinant subunit vaccine containing L1 capsid protein self-assembled into virus-like particles for types 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, 58). Recommended for males and females aged 9–26 years.

Adenovirus DNA Nonenveloped

  • Properties: Nonenveloped icosahedral capsid with distinct hemagglutinating fibers projecting from the 12 vertices. Linear double-stranded DNA encoding its own DNA polymerase. 41+ serotypes. Can establish latency in adenoids and tonsils.
  • Transmission: Respiratory droplets, fecal-oral (children), contaminated ophthalmic instruments (tonometers), swimming pools.
  • Clinical Findings: Acute respiratory disease and atypical pneumonia (serotypes 3, 4, 7, 21; prominent in military recruits); pharyngoconjunctival fever ("pink eye"); epidemic keratoconjunctivitis (types 8 and 19); acute hemorrhagic cystitis with dysuria and hematuria (types 11 and 21); infantile watery gastroenteritis (types 40 and 41).
  • Prevention: Live nonattenuated oral enteric-coated vaccines against serotypes 4, 7, and 21 (military personnel only).

Parvovirus B19 DNA Nonenveloped

  • Properties: Smallest naked DNA virus (22 nm); contains single-stranded linear DNA. Requires host cell in S phase to replicate because it relies entirely on host cell DNA polymerase. Binds erythrocyte P blood group antigen.
  • Clinical Findings:
    • Erythema Infectiosum (Fifth Disease / "Slapped Cheeks" Syndrome): Bright red maculopapular flush on cheeks followed by lacy, reticular erythematous rash on trunk/limbs; low fever.
    • Aplastic Crisis: Severe profound depletion of RBC precursors in patients with baseline chronic hemolytic anemias (sickle cell disease, thalassemia, spherocytosis).
    • Hydrops Fetalis: Infection of seronegative mother during second trimester leads to transplacental transmission, severe fetal anemia, congestive heart failure, and massive fetal hydrops. (First trimester leads to fetal death).
    • Immune Complex Arthritis: Symmetrical polyarthritis of small hand and foot joints in adult women, mimicking rheumatoid arthritis.
    • Chronic Infection: Persistent pure red cell aplasia and cytopenias in AIDS and immunosuppressed patients (treated with pooled IVIG).
  • Diagnosis: IgM antibodies against B19; PCR for viral DNA in blood or amniotic fluid.

Polyomaviruses (JC Virus & BK Virus) DNA Nonenveloped

  • JC Virus: Re-activates during severe T-cell deficiency (AIDS, cancer chemotherapy, natalizumab for multiple sclerosis). Causes Progressive Multifocal Leukoencephalopathy (PML): demyelination of white matter due to lytic infection of oligodendrocytes; causes visual deficits, mental status changes, ataxia, rapidly fatal within 6 months. Non-inflammatory (no pleocytosis). Diagnosed by PCR of CSF or brain biopsy.
  • BK Virus: Asymptomatically latent in kidneys; reactivates in renal transplant recipients, producing allograft nephropathy and ureteral stenosis. Asymptomatically shed in urine in immunosuppressed and late-stage pregnant women.
  • Merkel Cell Polyomavirus (MCPV): Integrated DNA causes Merkel cell neuroendocrine carcinoma of sun-exposed skin in elderly/immunosuppressed individuals via large T antigen inhibition of p53/Rb.

6. RNA Enveloped Viruses

Orthomyxoviruses: Influenza Virus RNA Enveloped

  • Properties: Helical nucleocapsid with an envelope containing hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) spikes. Internal genome contains 8 segments of negative-polarity ssRNA. RNA-dependent RNA polymerase is complexed to each segment. Replicates uniquely with an essential nuclear step where it engages in cap-snatching from host cell pre-mRNAs.
  • Envelope Glycoproteins:
    • Hemagglutinin (HA): Binds to host cell sialic (neuraminic) acid receptors; initiates membrane fusion following cleavage by host respiratory proteases. Target of neutralizing antibodies.
    • Neuraminidase (NA): Cleaves terminal sialic acid to liberate budding progeny virions; degrades respiratory mucus blanket. Target of oseltamivir and zanamivir.
    • M2 Matrix Protein: Forms an ion channel in the envelope to pump protons into endosomes, releasing the nucleocapsid into cytoplasm. Blocked by amantadine/rimantadine.
  • Antigenic Variation:
    • Antigenic Shift: Major genetic reassortment where entire genome RNA segments are exchanged between animal (avian/swine) and human strains co-infecting a host cell. Occurs only in Influenza A; causes pandemics.
    • Antigenic Drift: Minor mutations in HA or NA genes produced by error-prone RNA polymerase. Occurs in Influenza A and B; causes annual seasonal epidemics.
  • Pathogenesis & Clinicals: Necrosis of ciliated epithelium in larynx, trachea, and bronchi with intense systemic symptoms (high fever, severe myalgias, headache) caused by circulating IL-6, IL-8, and TNF. Major complications: secondary bacterial pneumonia (S. aureus, S. pneumoniae) and Reye’s syndrome with aspirin use.
  • Treatment & Prevention: Neuraminidase inhibitors (Oseltamivir, Zanamivir, Peramivir) and cap-snatching ribonuclease inhibitor Baloxavir marboxil. Vaccines include formal-inactivated egg-grown killed vaccine (trivalent or quadrivalent), cell-culture inactivated (Flucelvax), recombinant HA subunit (Flublok), and cold-adapted live attenuated intranasal vaccine (FluMist; replicates at 33°C in nose, not 37°C in lungs).

Paramyxoviruses RNA Enveloped

Nonsegmented, negative-strand ssRNA viruses with helical nucleocapsids, an envelope, and an RNA polymerase in the virion. Replicate strictly in cytoplasm and express a Fusion (F) protein that mediates cell fusion into multinucleated giant cells (syncytia).

  • Parainfluenza Virus: Four serotypes. Serotypes 1 and 2 are the leading cause of Croup (acute laryngotracheobronchitis) in young children, presenting with a harsh, barking cough and inspiratory stridor. Serotype 3 causes bronchiolitis and pneumonia.
  • Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV): Surface spikes contain F protein (no hemagglutinin or NA). Leading cause of infant pneumonia and bronchiolitis. Maternal antibodies may exacerbate disease through immune-complex deposition. Treated with aerosolized ribavirin in severe infant disease. Prophylaxis with monoclonal antibody against F protein (Palivizumab) in premature/immunocompromised infants.
  • Human Metapneumovirus (HMPV): Resembles RSV clinically; causes bronchiolitis, cough, wheezing in young children.
  • Measles (Rubeola) Virus: Single serotype; transmitted via airborne droplets.
    • Pathogenesis: Replicates in respiratory epithelium → primary viremia → reticuloendothelial system → secondary viremia → skin. Rash is mediated by CD8+ T cells attacking virus-infected endothelial cells in skin vessels. Transiently suppresses cellular immunity by downregulating IL-12 via CD46, which can reactivate dormant tuberculosis.
    • Clinical Features: High fever, the "three Cs" (cough, coryza, conjunctivitis), Koplik's spots (bright red oral mucosa with white center), followed by descending maculopapular morbilliform rash including palms and soles. Complications: acute encephalitis, subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE; fatal slow brain degenerations years later). High fatality in vitamin A deficiency.
    • Prevention: Live, attenuated MMR vaccine at 12–15 months, boosted at 4–6 years.
  • Mumps Virus: Infects parotid salivary glands (bilateral tender enlargement aggravated by sour liquids). Complications: aseptic meningitis, acute painful orchitis in postpubertal males (tunica albuginea swelling can cause pressure necrosis and sterility if bilateral). Prevented by live attenuated MMR vaccine.
  • Hendra & Nipah Viruses: Zoonotic paramyxoviruses from fruit bats; Nipah causes high-mortality severe encephalitis in pig handlers in Southeast Asia.

Coronaviruses & SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) RNA Enveloped

  • General Characteristics: Helical nucleocapsid, single-stranded nonsegmented positive-polarity RNA. Virion lacks RNA polymerase. Notable for large club-shaped surface glycoprotein spikes forming a crown (corona). Genome translates two polyproteins cleaved by viral proteases; produces a characteristic nested set of subgenomic mRNAs. Buds from endoplasmic reticulum. Four common serotypes cause the common cold; three zoonotic strains cause severe lower respiratory tract pneumonia: SARS-CoV, MERS-CoV, and SARS-CoV-2.
  • SARS-CoV-2 Virology: Binds host ACE-2 protein via receptor-binding domain of spike protein. Cleaved by host surface serine protease TMPRSS-2 to trigger viral-cell envelope fusion. Also interacts with neuropilin-1 (NRP-1). Transmitted via respiratory droplets, aerosols, and fomites. Shedding begins 2–3 days before symptom onset (prominent asymptomatic transmission).
  • Clinical Manifestations: Incubation mean 5 days. Fever, dry cough, shortness of breath, severe fatigue, anosmia (loss of smell), dysgeusia (altered taste), bilateral ground-glass pulmonary opacities, hypoxemic respiratory failure, Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS).
    • Extra-respiratory Findings: Endothelial cell destruction, microangiopathy, capillary thrombosis, stroke, myocardial injury, acute renal failure, "COVID toes", and Pediatric Multisystem Inflammatory Syndrome (MIS-C).
    • Cytokine Release Syndrome (Cytokine Storm): Marked elevations of IL-1, IL-6, TNF, and bradykinin driving tissue devastation.
    • Post-Acute Sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 (PASC / Long COVID): Persistent chronic fatigue, dyspnea, brain fog, and arthralgias.
  • Management: Remdesivir (inhibits RNA-dependent RNA polymerase); Dexamethasone (suppresses cytokine storm mortality); Baricitinib (JAK inhibitor); Tocilizumab (monoclonal antibody targeting IL-6 receptor); Monoclonal neutralizing antibody cocktails (Casirivimab/Imdevimab, Bamlanivimab/Etesevimab).
  • Vaccines: mRNA vaccines encoding spike protein enclosed in lipid nanoparticles (Pfizer/BioNTech, Moderna); Adenoviral-vectored DNA vaccines (Johnson & Johnson Ad26; Oxford/AstraZeneca).

Flaviviruses RNA Enveloped

Icosahedral nucleocapsid, nonsegmented positive-strand ssRNA. Translates a single polyprotein cleaved by viral protease.

  • Hepatitis C Virus (HCV): Blood-borne pathogen (IV drug abuse; historical transfusions). High mutation rate in envelope glycoprotein (quasispecies; lacks proofreading in RNA polymerase).
    • Pathogenesis: Cytotoxic T-cell destruction of hepatocytes; viral protease suppresses host interferon induction. >75% develop chronic infection; leads to cirrhosis, liver failure, and Hepatocellular Carcinoma (no viral oncogene; driven by continuous regeneration). Causes essential mixed cryoglobulinemia, membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis, neuropathy.
    • Treatment: Direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) achieve virological cure without interferon: Sofosbuvir (polymerase), Ledipasvir/Velpatasvir (NS5A), Glecaprevir/Grazoprevir (protease). (Warning: DAA therapy may reactivate latent HBV).
  • Yellow Fever Virus: Transmitted by Aedes aegypti (urban cycle; humans reservoir) and Haemagogus (jungle cycle; monkeys reservoir). Sudden fever, bradycardia, jaundice, renal dysfunction, and massive GI hemorrhage with "black vomit." Prevented by live attenuated 17D vaccine.
  • Dengue Virus: Four serotypes. Transmitted by Aedes aegypti. Classic dengue ("breakbone fever"): severe headache, retro-orbital pain, severe arthralgia/myalgias, maculopapular rash. Dengue Hemorrhagic Fever / Shock: Occurs when infected with a second heterologous serotype; pre-existing cross-reacting non-neutralizing antibodies promote antibody-dependent enhancement into macrophages, causing massive cytokine storm, vascular leakage, thrombocytopenia, and shock. Prevented by tetravalent recombinant Dengvaxia (restricted to individuals with documented prior infection).
  • Zika Virus: Transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes; also transplacentally and sexually via semen. Causes mild rash, low fever, arthralgia, conjunctivitis. Strong teratogen: maternal first-trimester infection causes congenital microcephaly, cerebral calcifications, visual and hearing impairment. Associated with Guillain-Barré syndrome.
  • West Nile Virus (WNV): Reservoir in wild birds (crows, sparrows); transmitted by Culex mosquitoes. Leading cause of arboviral neuroinvasive encephalitis/meningitis in the US, predominantly in elderly patients (>60 yrs).
  • St. Louis Encephalitis (SLE) & Japanese Encephalitis: SLE transmitted by Culex in urban wastewater areas. Japanese Encephalitis is the major cause of epidemic encephalitis in rural Asian rice fields (pigs and birds reservoir; prevented by killed vaccine).

Togaviruses (Rubiviruses & Alphaviruses) RNA Enveloped

  • Rubella Virus: Enveloped positive-strand ssRNA with icosahedral capsid. Respiratory transmission.
    • Postnatal Rubella: Mild 3-day maculopapular rash beginning on face and moving down, posterior auricular lymphadenopathy, post-infectious polyarthritis in adult females.
    • Congenital Rubella Syndrome: Maternal infection during first trimester causes severe fetal anomalies: patent ductus arteriosus, cataracts, sensorineural deafness, mental retardation. Congenital infants shed infectious virus for months. Prevented by live attenuated vaccine (MMR).
  • Alphaviruses: Enveloped arboviruses.
    • Eastern Equine Encephalitis (EEE): Swamp mosquito vector (Culiseta); wild bird reservoir. Causes severe fulminant necrotizing encephalitis with ~50% mortality.
    • Western Equine Encephalitis (WEE): Transmitted by Culex; milder course.
    • Chikungunya Virus: Transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes. Sudden high fever, maculopapular rash, and crippling, symmetric, bilateral small-joint polyarthralgias lasting months.

Rhabdoviruses & Filoviruses RNA Enveloped

  • Rabies Virus: Bullet-shaped helical enveloped virion containing negative-polarity nonsegmented ssRNA and virion RNA polymerase.
    • Transmission & Neurotropism: Transmitted via animal bites (bats, skunks, raccoons in the US; feral dogs in developing nations) or bat cave aerosols. Attaches to cellular acetylcholine receptors at neuromuscular junction, ascends peripheral nerve axons retrograde without a viremic stage into the CNS. Produces neuronal death and eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusion bodies (Negri bodies) in hippocampal pyramidal cells.
    • Clinical Features: Long incubation (2 to 16+ weeks). Paresthesias at bite site. Furious form (80%): agitation, seizures, spasms of pharyngeal musculature on swallowing liquids (hydrophobia). Dumb form (20%): ascending flaccid paralysis. Almost universally fatal once clinical symptoms develop.
    • Postexposure Prophylaxis: Immediate thorough wound cleansing; passive-active immunization combining human rabies immune globulin (RIG; infiltrated at bite site) and inactivated human diploid cell vaccine (HDCV; 4–5 doses given intramuscularly at a distant site).
  • Ebola & Marburg Viruses (Filoviruses): Enveloped, extraordinarily long thread-like filamentous negative-strand ssRNA viruses with virion RNA polymerase.
    • Pathogenesis: Natural reservoir suspected to be fruit bats. Person-to-person spread via direct contact with blood/body secretions and contaminated needles; virus persists long-term in semen of male survivors. Glycoprotein causes cytotoxicity to vascular endothelial cells, accompanied by multiorgan necrosis, massive thrombocytopenia, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), widespread internal/external hemorrhage, shock, and multiorgan failure (up to 90% mortality). Cultured only in BSL-4. Post-Ebola syndrome includes uveitis and arthralgia.
    • Prevention: Recombinant live vesicular stomatitis virus-vectored vaccine expressing Ebola surface glycoprotein (rZEBOV).

Arenaviruses & Bunyaviruses RNA Enveloped

  • Arenaviruses: Enveloped, helical, containing two segments of single-stranded ambisense circular RNA. Virions feature nonfunctional sandy granules (host ribosomes). Chronic asymptomatic infection in rodents; transmitted via inhalation of dried rodent excreta.
    • Lassa Fever Virus: Endemic in West Africa (Mastomys rodent reservoir). Severe multiorgan hemorrhagic fever with vascular collapse (20% fatality). Treated with ribavirin.
    • Lymphocytic Choriomeningitis Virus (LCMV): Domestic mouse reservoir; causes aseptic choriomeningitis. Famous prototype for cell-mediated immunopathology (meningitis caused by host T-cell attack). Transplant transmission carries high mortality.
    • Other Arenaviruses: Lujo virus (Zambia), Whitewater Arroyo virus (US wood rats; ARDS/hemorrhagic fever), Tacaribe complex (Junin, Machupo, Sabia; South American hemorrhagic fevers).
  • Bunyaviruses: Enveloped, helical, 3 circular negative-strand RNA segments (L, M, S).
    • Hantaviruses: Roboviruses (rodent-borne without insect vector). Hantaan virus causes Korean hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome. Sin Nombre Virus (endemic in US deer mice Peromyscus) causes Hantavirus Pulmonary Syndrome: sudden acute interstitial pulmonary edema, rapid respiratory failure, shock (35% fatality).
    • California Encephalitis / La Crosse Virus: Transmitted by forest mosquito Aedes triseriatus from rodents; causes pediatric encephalitis in north-central US. Transovarially passed in mosquitoes.
    • Crimean-Congo Hemorrhagic Virus (CCHV): Transmitted by Hyalomma ticks and nosocomial blood contact; severe hemorrhagic fever with hepatic necrosis.
    • Heartland Virus: Lone Star tick (Amblyomma); causes fever, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia in Missouri.

Retroviruses & Hepatitis D RNA Enveloped

Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) & Human T-Cell Leukemia Virus (HTLV) are enveloped retroviruses with diploid single-stranded positive-polarity RNA genomes and virion-associated reverse transcriptase, integrase, and protease.

HIV Structural & Regulatory Genes
  • gag: Encodes capsid core protein p24 (primary serologic diagnostic marker), p17 matrix, and nucleocapsid p7.
  • pol: Encodes reverse transcriptase (RNA-dependent DNA polymerase + RNase H), integrase, and viral protease.
  • env: Encodes gp160 precursor, cleaved into surface gp120 (binds CD4 receptor and CCR5/CXCR4 coreceptors) and transmembrane gp41 (mediates viral-cell membrane fusion). Rapid antigenic variation in gp120 V3 loop.
  • tat & rev: Essential for replication. Tat transactivates viral transcription at the TAR element on 5' LTR. Rev coordinates cytoplasmic transport of unspliced/partially spliced structural mRNAs.
  • Accessory Genes: nef (downregulates cell-surface CD4 and Class I MHC; triggers apoptosis of uninfected T cells), vif (inactivates innate antiviral cytidine deaminase APOBEC3G), vpr, vpu.
  • HIV Pathogenesis: Depletes CD4+ helper T cells via caspase-mediated apoptosis and syncytia formation. Early loss of mucosal Th17 cells in GI tract allows bacterial translocation and enterobacterial sepsis. Latent provirus persists permanently integrated in resting memory CD4+ T cells. AIDS diagnosed when CD4 count ≤200 cells/μL or opportunistic AIDS-defining illness develops (PCP, Kaposi's sarcoma, cryptococcal meningitis, CMV retinitis, esophageal candidiasis, toxoplasmosis).
  • HIV Diagnosis: 4th-generation "Combo" immunoassay detects p24 antigen and HIV-1/HIV-2 antibodies; confirmed by HIV-1/HIV-2 differentiation assay and quantitative PCR viral load.
  • HIV Prophylaxis: PrEP (daily oral Tenofovir/Emtricitabine [Truvada or Descovy] or bimonthly injectable Cabotegravir); PEP (28-day course of Tenofovir + Emtricitabine + Dolutegravir within 72 hours of exposure).
  • Human T-Cell Leukemia Virus (HTLV-1): Does not lyse T cells; immortalizes them. Tax protein acts as a transcriptional transactivator inducing IL-2 and IL-2 receptors, leading to Adult T-cell Leukemia/Lymphoma (ATL) with flower-shaped T cells, hypercalcemia, and osteolytic bone lesions. Also causes HTLV-Associated Myelopathy (HAM) / Tropical Spastic Paraparesis (progressive, non-remitting demyelinating upper motor neuron spastic weakness of lower limbs).
  • Hepatitis D Virus (HDV, Delta Agent): Defective virus containing tiny, closed circular negative-strand RNA encoding a single internal delta antigen. Lacks an envelope gene; strictly dependent on coinfection with HBV to supply an outer coat of HBsAg. Co-infection with HBV produces severe acute hepatitis; superinfection of an established chronic HBV carrier induces catastrophic fulminant hepatitis and accelerated cirrhosis. Prevented by HBV vaccination.

7. RNA Nonenveloped (Naked) Viruses

Picornaviruses: Enteroviruses & Rhinoviruses RNA Nonenveloped

Naked icosahedral virions with single-stranded linear positive-polarity RNA. Translates a single large polyprotein cleaved by viral proteases.

  • Poliovirus: Three serotypes. Fecal-oral transmission; replicates in lymphoid tissue of oropharynx and Peyer's patches → primary viremia → secondary viremia → invades central nervous system. Selectively destroys motor neurons in the anterior horns of the spinal cord, causing asymmetric flaccid paralysis without sensory loss. Brainstem involvement causes bulbar polio and fatal respiratory arrest.
    • Vaccines: Inactivated Polio Vaccine (eIPV / Salk) given by injection induces humoral IgG; prevents CNS paralytic disease but does not generate secretory IgA (vaccinated host can still transmit wild virus). Live Attenuated Polio Vaccine (OPV / Sabin) taken orally induces gut mucosal IgA and IgG, interrupting fecal-oral transmission; carries rare risk of reversion to virulence (vaccine-derived polioviruses, VDPV).
  • Coxsackieviruses:
    • Group A: Herpangina (painful oropharyngeal vesicles), Hand-foot-and-mouth disease (vesicular lesions on palms, soles, and oral mucosa), acute hemorrhagic conjunctivitis (A24).
    • Group B: Pleurodynia (Bornholm disease / "devil's grip": severe paroxysmal intercostal muscle pain), Acute myocarditis and pericarditis (can lead to dilated cardiomyopathy and heart failure). Both groups cause aseptic meningitis.
  • Echoviruses: Leading cause of viral (aseptic) meningitis; infantile diarrhea and febrile exanthems.
  • Enterovirus D68, 70, 71: EV-D68 causes severe childhood respiratory disease and acute flaccid myelitis; EV-70 causes acute hemorrhagic conjunctivitis; EV-71 causes meningoencephalitis and flaccid paralysis.
  • Hepatitis A Virus (HAV): Enterovirus 72; single serotype. Transmitted fecal-orally via contaminated water, raw shellfish (oysters). Replicates in gut and spreads via portal vein to liver; hepatocyte destruction caused by host cytotoxic T cells. Short incubation (3–4 weeks). Does not cause chronic hepatitis, carrier state, or HCC. Diagnosed by serum IgM anti-HAV. Prevented by formalin-inactivated HAV vaccine.
  • Rhinovirus: Over 100 serotypes; acid-labile (destroyed by gastric acid; does not infect GI tract). Grows optimally at 33°C (adapted to upper nasal mucosa; does not infect lower lung). Most common cause of the common cold (coryza, sneezing, rhinorrhea). Attaches to ICAM-1. Transmitted by aerosols and hand contact with fomites.

Caliciviruses, Hepeviruses, Reoviruses & Others RNA Nonenveloped

  • Norovirus (Calicivirus): Naked positive-strand ssRNA with cup-shaped surface depressions. Extremely low infectious dose; resistant to chlorine disinfection and environmental drying. Leading cause of nonbacterial epidemic gastroenteritis in adults and children worldwide (outbreaks on cruise ships, schools, nursing homes). Causes sudden watery, nonbloody diarrhea, prominent vomiting, cramping; no fecal RBCs or WBCs. Diagnosed by stool RT-PCR.
  • Hepatitis E Virus (HEV, Hepevirus): Waterborne enterically transmitted hepatitis; waterborne outbreaks in Asia, Africa, Mexico. Clinically resembles HAV, but causes catastrophic fulminant hepatic failure with high mortality (up to 20%) in pregnant women. Chronic infection can occur in solid organ transplant recipients.
  • Rotavirus (Reovirus): Double-layered icosahedral capsid containing 11 segments of double-stranded RNA and virion RNA-dependent RNA polymerase. Leading cause of severe, dehydrating, nonbloody watery diarrhea in young infants worldwide. NSP4 enterotoxin acts as a viral pore-forming secretagogue triggering intracellular calcium release and fluid efflux. Prevented by live oral reassortant vaccines (RotaTeq pentavalent bovine-human reassortant; Rotarix attenuated human strain). Increases risk of intussusception.
  • Colorado Tick Fever Virus: Arbovirus member of Reoviridae (Orbivirus). Transmitted by Dermacentor andersoni wood ticks in the Rocky Mountains; infects erythrocyte precursors; causes fever, retro-orbital pain, severe myalgias.
  • Astrovirus & Sapporo Virus: Naked positive-strand RNA viruses causing watery pediatric diarrhea.

II. Medical Mycology

1. Fungal Structure, Pathogenesis & Antifungals

Fungal Cell Structure vs. Bacteria
  • Eukaryotic Nature: True nucleus with nuclear membrane, endoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria, and 80S ribosomes. Heterotrophs requiring preformed organic carbon. Strictly nonmotile; obligate or facultative aerobes (no obligate anaerobes).
  • Cell Wall: Contains Chitin (chains of N-acetylglucosamine) and β-glucan (polymers of D-glucose). Lacks peptidoglycan; therefore entirely insensitive to penicillins, cephalosporins, or vancomycin.
  • Cell Membrane: Contains Ergosterol as the principal sterol (contrasting with human cholesterol). This difference provides selective toxicity for polyenes and azoles.
  • Morphologic Forms:
    • Yeasts: Single cells reproducing by asexual blastoconidia budding. (May fail to detach, forming elongated chains called pseudohyphae).
    • Molds: Branching multicellular filaments called hyphae forming a mat-like mycelium. Hyphae may be septate (transverse cross-walls) or nonseptate (coenocytic, multinucleated).
    • Thermal Dimorphism: Fungi that grow as environmental saprophytic molds at ambient temperatures (25°C), but differentiate into budding yeasts or parasitic spherules in mammalian tissues at body temperature (37°C). (Applies to Coccidioides, Histoplasma, Blastomyces, Paracoccidioides, Sporothrix).

Fungal Toxins & Allergies (Mycotoxicoses)

  • Amanitin & Phalloidin: Produced by Amanita phalloides mushrooms. Amanitin halts host cellular protein synthesis by specifically inhibiting human RNA polymerase II, leading to acute fulminant centrilobular liver necrosis and death.
  • Aflatoxin: Coumarin derivatives synthesized by Aspergillus flavus on moldy stored grains and peanuts. Metabolized in the liver to reactive epoxides that induce specific transversions in codon 249 of the p53 tumor suppressor gene, directly driving Hepatocellular Carcinoma.
  • Ergotamine & LSD: Alkaloids produced by Claviceps purpurea infecting rye grain; cause severe peripheral vasospasm, gangrene, and hallucinations.
  • Fungal Allergies: Inhaled conidia (especially Aspergillus fumigatus) trigger IgE-mediated immediate hypersensitivity, manifesting as allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) with bronchospasm, peripheral eosinophilia, and pulmonary infiltrates.

2. Cutaneous & Subcutaneous Mycoses

Dermatophytoses (Ringworm) Cutaneous Mold

Caused by three genera of non-dimorphic molds: Trichophyton, Epidermophyton, and Microsporum. They live only in nonviable keratinized skin, hair, and nails, sustained by keratinase. Do not invade deep viable tissue.

  • Transmission: Direct human-to-human contact; Microsporum also transmitted from domestic dogs and cats.
  • Clinical Forms:
    • Tinea corporis: Annular, pruritic, erythematous scaling rings with raised advancing border and central clearing.
    • Tinea pedis (Athlete's foot) & Tinea cruris (Jock itch): Maceration, scaling, intense itching.
    • Tinea capitis: Scalp ringworm. T. tonsurans causes endothrix infection; T. schoenleinii causes favus (crusting); can trigger a severe hypersensitivity boggy pustular nodule called a kerion. Microsporum lesions fluoresce brilliant green under Wood’s UV lamp.
    • Tinea unguium (Onychomycosis): Thickened, brittle, discolored nails.
    • Dermatophytid ("Id") Reaction: Circulating fungal antigens trigger sterile, intensely pruritic vesicles on distant fingers/body sites.
  • Diagnosis: 10% KOH preparation of scrapings demonstrating branching septate hyphae; culture on Sabouraud’s dextrose agar.
  • Treatment: Topical terbinafine, miconazole, clotrimazole, undecylenic acid. Oral terbinafine or griseofulvin for hair and nail involvement.

Tinea Versicolor & Tinea Nigra Superficial

  • Tinea (Pityriasis) Versicolor: Caused by Malassezia species. Superficial cosmetic infection characterized by hypopigmented or hyperpigmented scaly macules on the chest and back, accentuated in warm humid seasons and sun exposure. KOH mount displays a diagnostic mixture of short curved septate hyphae and round budding yeasts ("spaghetti and meatballs"). Treated with topical miconazole or selenium sulfide; oral fluconazole for extensive disease.
  • Tinea Nigra: Caused by Cladosporium werneckii (soil mold). Produces dark brownish/black solitary painless macules on the palms or soles due to melanin production in the hyphae. Treated with topical keratolytic agents (salicylic acid).

Subcutaneous Mycoses: Sporotrichosis, Chromomycosis, Mycetoma Subcutaneous

  • Sporotrichosis (Sporothrix schenckii): Thermally dimorphic fungus found on soil, sphagnum moss, and vegetation.
    • Transmission: Traumatic subcutaneous implantation, classically by a rose thorn puncture in gardeners and florists.
    • Clinical Findings: Local primary painless erythematous papule/pustule that ulcerates, followed by a linear succession of secondary ulcerating subcutaneous nodules along draining lymphatic channels. Disseminated disease in AIDS patients.
    • Diagnosis: Biopsy reveals cigar-shaped budding yeasts at 37°C. Culture at 25°C develops mold producing delicate branching hyphae with clusters of pear-shaped conidia in a characteristic daisy-petal arrangement at the tips of conidiophores.
    • Treatment: Oral Itraconazole.
  • Chromomycosis: Caused by dematiaceous (dark-pigmented melanin-producing) soil fungi (Fonsecaea, Phialophora, Cladosporium) introduced through puncture wounds in barefoot agricultural workers in the tropics. Characterized by slowly developing, wart-like, verrucous crusted cauliflower lesions along draining lymphatics. Biopsy displays dark brown round sclerotic bodies (Medlar bodies) inside giant cells. Treated with flucytosine or thiabendazole and surgical excision.
  • Mycetoma (Madura Foot): Chronic suppurative infection caused by soil fungi (Petriellidium, Madurella) or actinomycetes (Nocardia). Traumatic foot puncture develops draining sinus tracts discharging compact colored granules composed of fungal masses. Fungal mycetoma is refractory to medical therapy; requires wide surgical resection.

3. Systemic (Endemic) Mycoses

Defining Hallmarks of Systemic Mycoses

Dimorphic fungi inhabiting the soil in specific geographic zones as molds. Transmitted via inhalation of airborne asexual conidia/arthrospores into the lungs. No human-to-human transmission. Inside alveolar tissues, they transform into specialized parasitic forms (yeasts or spherules). Host defense relies on granuloma formation mediated by CD4+ Th1 cellular immunity.

Coccidioides immitis & C. posadasii Arid Southwestern US (California San Joaquin Valley, Arizona, New Mexico, West Texas) & Latin America. Mold forming alternating barrel-shaped rectangular arthrospores separated by empty cells. Extremely infectious. Thick-walled, doubly-refractive spherules (30–60 μm) packed with endospores. AIDS patients: pancytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, painful tongue/mucosal ulcers. Urine antigen test useful.
Blastomyces dermatitidis Eastern & Central North America bordering Ohio, Mississippi, St. Lawrence River systems, Great Lakes. Moist organic soil. Mold producing slender hyphae with small pear-shaped microconidia. Large, thick-walled yeasts with a single, broad-based bud and doubly-refractive wall. Chronic granulomatous lung disease; disseminates to skin (ulcerative verrucous granulomas with microabscesses) and osteolytic bone lesions.
Paracoccidioides brasiliensis Rural Latin America, especially Brazil and Central America. Mold with chlamydospores. Large thick-walled yeast with multiple circumferential buds resembling a "ship's captain's wheel". Chronic granulomatous ulceration of oral and nasal mucosa, destructive facial lesions, massive regional lymphadenopathy.
Therapy for Endemic Systemic Mycoses:
  • Mild to Moderate Pulmonary Disease: Oral Itraconazole (or fluconazole for Coccidioides).
  • Severe, Cavitary, or Disseminated Disease: Intravenous Amphotericin B (liposomal formulation preferred in renal compromise), followed by prolonged azole step-down maintenance.
  • Fungal Meningitis: Oral/IV Fluconazole (high CSF penetration). Coccidioidal meningitis requires lifelong daily fluconazole suppression to prevent lethal relapses; intrathecal amphotericin B used when azoles fail.

4. Opportunistic Fungal Pathogens

Candida Species Yeast / Opportunistic

  • Properties: Normal flora of human skin, mouth, GI tract, and vagina. Oval budding yeast that forms pseudohyphae (chains of unseparated elongated blastoconidia) and true hyphae when invading tissues. C. albicans specifically forms germ tubes when incubated in mammalian serum at 37°C for 3 hours, and produces terminal thick-walled chlamydospores at 20°C. (C. dubliniensis also forms chlamydospores but fails to grow at 42°C).
  • Species Diversity: C. glabrata (frequent azole resistance), C. krusei (intrinsic fluconazole resistance), C. parapsilosis (catheter/line infections), C. tropicalis, and Candida auris (multi-drug and pan-resistant emerging hospital pathogen).
  • Predisposing Factors & Syndromes:
    • Local Host Impairment: Broad-spectrum antibiotics eradicate protective Lactobacillus flora, raising vaginal pH and causing vulvovaginitis (cheesy discharge, pruritus). Inhaled or systemic corticosteroids, diabetes, and HIV cause oral thrush (white curd-like pseudomembranes easily scraped off leaving an erythematous bleeding base). Dishwashers develop chronic paronychia. Diaper rash occurs in unwashed wet infants.
    • Impaired Cell-Mediated Immunity: Mutations in IL-17 signaling and severe T-cell deficiency cause Chronic Mucocutaneous Candidiasis (CMC) with disfiguring nail, skin, and mucosal vegetative plaques.
    • Impaired Phagocytic / Barrier Defenses (Neutropenia, Catheters, IV Drug Abuse): Causes candidemia, right-sided tricuspid endocarditis, endophthalmitis, and metastatic necrotic subcutaneous microabscesses. Biofilm formation on intravascular catheters is crucial.
  • Diagnosis: Calcofluor white stain; KOH prep; germ tube test; MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry; blood cultures.
  • Treatment: Fluconazole for thrush, esophagitis, vaginitis; Echinocandins (Caspofungin, Micafungin) or Amphotericin B for candidemia, neutropenic fungemia, and C. glabrata / C. auris infections. Topical nystatin or clotrimazole for superficial skin/oral disease.

Cryptococcus neoformans & C. gattii Encapsulated Yeast

  • Properties: Monomorphic yeast with a single, narrow-based bud encased in an exceptionally wide, gelatinous polysaccharide capsule. Not thermally dimorphic. Found abundantly worldwide in soil enriched with pigeon excrement. Inhaled into alveoli. (C. gattii is associated with eucalyptus trees, infecting immunocompetent hosts).
  • Pathogenesis & Manifestations: Inhalation causes primary subclinical lung nodules. Disseminates hematogenously to the brain in individuals with impaired cell-mediated immunity (AIDS with CD4 <100). Causes indolent, subacute cryptococcal meningitis (headache, low fever, confusion, increased intracranial pressure) with minimal meningeal inflammation. Disseminated cutaneous papules resembling molluscum.
  • Laboratory Diagnosis:
    • India Ink Wet Mount of CSF: Outlines the wide, clear, unstained capsule against a dark particulate field.
    • Cryptococcal Antigen (CRAG) Test: Latex particle agglutination test detecting capsular glucuronoxylomannan polysaccharide in CSF and serum (highly sensitive and specific).
    • Histology: Mucicarmine stain stains the capsule bright red; Methenamine silver and PAS stains visualize yeast cells. Highly mucoid colonies on Sabouraud's agar.
  • Treatment: Induction with IV Amphotericin B plus oral Flucytosine for ≥2 weeks, followed by consolidation and lifelong maintenance with oral Fluconazole in AIDS patients until CD4 counts rise. Immune Reconstitution Inflammatory Syndrome (IRIS) must be managed if antiretrovirals are initiated.

Aspergillus fumigatus & A. flavus Monomorphic Mold

  • Properties: Monomorphic saprophytic environmental molds (not dimorphic). Composed of parallel-walled septate hyphae with progressive narrow, V-shaped (45° dichotomous) branching. Airborne conidia form radiating chains on an expanded conidiophore.
  • Pathogenesis & Clinical Spectra:
    • Aspergilloma (Fungus Ball): Conidia inhaled into pre-existing tuberculous or histoplasmic lung cavities germinate into an entangled mass of hyphae, fibrin, and blood debris. Appears on chest X-ray as a gravity-dependent crescentic radiopaque mobile mass shifting with patient position. Presents with massive hemoptysis.
    • Invasive Aspergillosis: Severe angioinvasive necrotizing pneumonia with hemorrhagic infarction and dissemination (brain abscesses) in profoundly neutropenic leukemia and bone marrow transplant patients.
    • Allergic Bronchopulmonary Aspergillosis (ABPA): Colonization of bronchi in asthmatics triggers IgE-mediated wheezing, expectoration of brown bronchial mucus plugs, eosinophilia, and elevated serum IgE.
    • Fungal Sinusitis & Keratitis: Invasive paranasal sinus destruction; corneal ulceration after plant trauma.
  • Diagnosis: Biopsy demonstrating tissue-invading 45° branching septate hyphae; culture; serum galactomannan antigen assay by ELISA.
  • Treatment: Voriconazole is the drug of choice for invasive disease. Liposomal Amphotericin B, Isavuconazole, Caspofungin. Surgical resection for pulmonary aspergilloma. Corticosteroids plus oral itraconazole for ABPA.

Mucormycosis (Mucor, Rhizopus, Absidia) Nonseptate Mold

  • Properties: Saprophytic environmental molds with broad, irregular, ribbon-like nonseptate (coenocytic) hyphae branching at wide right angles (90°). Asexual spores enclosed within a spherical sac called a sporangium on an upright stalk.
  • Pathogenesis & Predisposing Factors: Spores inhaled into the respiratory tract. Markedly predilected for patients with diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), profound neutropenia, extensive burns, and deferoxamine iron chelation therapy. The mold proliferates rapidly in high-glucose, acidic, ketone-rich environments.
  • Angioinvasive Destruction: Hyphae directly invade walls of blood vessels, inducing extensive arterial thrombosis, infarction, and rapidly spreading black necrotic eschars.
    • Rhinocerebral Mucormycosis: Spores germinate in paranasal sinuses, invade the palate, orbit, cribriform plate, and frontal lobe of the brain. Presents with facial pain, bloody nasal discharge, proptosis, cavernous sinus thrombosis, and black necrotic tissue on the hard palate and turbinates.
    • Pulmonary & Gastrointestinal: Hemorrhagic infarction in neutropenic patients.
  • Diagnosis: Microscopic examination of fresh tissue biopsy showing broad, ribbon-like, nonseptate hyphae branching at 90° angles. (Difficult to culture because grinding breaks fragile nonseptate filaments).
  • Treatment: Emergency wide surgical debridement of necrotic tissues + high-dose IV Amphotericin B (liposomal) + reversal of underlying diabetic ketoacidosis. Posaconazole used as step-down/prophylaxis. Echinocandins are inactive.

Pneumocystis jiroveci Unclassified Yeast/Fungus

  • Unique Biology: Genetically classified as a fungus based on ribosomal RNA homology; however, membranes contain cholesterol instead of ergosterol. Entirely nonresponsive to polyenes and azoles. Does not grow on fungal culture media. Exhibits surface glycoprotein antigenic variation.
  • Pathogenesis: Inhaled into alveoli. In patients with severe CD4+ T-cell depression (<200/μL in AIDS), attachment triggers an intra-alveolar plasma-cell-rich foamy exudate, alveolar capillary block, severe impairment of oxygen diffusion, dyspnea, and hypoxia. Does not invade bronchial parenchyma. Untreated mortality ~100%.
  • Clinical Findings: Insidious onset of fever, nonproductive dry cough, severe dyspnea, tachypnea, and bilateral diffuse interstitial "ground-glass" perihilar infiltrates on chest X-ray.
  • Diagnosis: Examination of induced sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid stained with methenamine silver, Giemsa, or immunofluorescent monoclonal antibodies showing cup-shaped, collapsed spherical cysts with dark central nuclei. PCR assays.
  • Treatment & Chemoprophylaxis: Drug of choice is oral or IV Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX). Prophylaxis mandatory in all HIV patients when CD4 <200 cells/μL. Alternative drugs: Pentamidine, Atovaquone.

Fungi of Minor Importance

  • Talaromyces marneffei (Penicillium marneffei): Dimorphic fungus endemic in Southeast Asia (Thailand); reservoir in bamboo rats. In AIDS patients, causes disseminated mycobacterial-like disease with umbilicated cutaneous papules. Mold at 25°C secretes a distinctive rose-red diffusible pigment; yeast at 37°C divides by fission with a central transverse septum. Treated with amphotericin B followed by oral itraconazole.
  • Pseudallescheria boydii & Scedosporium apiospermum: Environmental molds with septate hyphae resembling Aspergillus in tissue. Causes solitary mycetoma in normal hosts, but devastating angioinvasive abscesses and pneumonia in immunocompromised hosts. Scedosporium is resistant to virtually all antifungal drugs (including amphotericin B). Treated with voriconazole or itraconazole.
  • Fusarium solani: Septate mold that produces sickle- or banana-shaped conidia. Invades blood vessels causing persistent fungemia and painful skin nodules in neutropenic patients. Outbreak of fungal keratitis linked to contact lens disinfectant. Treated with liposomal amphotericin B.
  • Microsporidia (Enterocytozoon bieneusi, Encephalitozoon intestinalis): Obligate intracellular, spore-forming eukaryotic organisms reclassified from protozoa to fungi. Spores (1–3 μm) possess a characteristic coiled polar tube that extrudes to inject infective sporoplasm directly into host cells. Cause severe, persistent, debilitating watery diarrhea and wasting in AIDS patients. Transmitted fecal-orally. Treated with Albendazole.
